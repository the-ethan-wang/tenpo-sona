The mixed strategy Nash equilibrium is one where both players cast rock, paper and scissors at a randomly chosen uniform 1/3 - 1/3 - 1/3 distribution. This is a Nash equilibrium as, holding player B's strategy constant as this distribution, player A never increases their expected payoff by switching their strategy. 

Let there be a strategy, $K$, such that $K_r$ represents the probability that the strategy throws rock, and $K_s$ and $K_p$ represent scissors and paper, and $0 <= K_r, K_s, K_p <= 1$ and $K_r + K_s + K_p = 1$.

Thus, a player's expected payoff while using strategy $K$ against the strategy of an even distribution can be expressed as $K_r\cdot(1/3-1/3+0)+K_p\cdot(1/3-1/3+0)+K_s\cdot(1/3-1/3+0)$  shown in the following table as totalling to 0 net payoff.

|         | $K_r$            | $K_p$            | $K_s$            | Total                         |
| ------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------- |
| R (1/3) | 0                | $\frac{K_p}{3}$  | $-\frac{K_s}{3}$ | $\frac{K_p}{3}-\frac{K_s}{3}$ |
| P (1/3) | $-\frac{K_r}{3}$ | 0                | $\frac{K_s}{3}$  | $\frac{K_s}{3}-\frac{K_r}{3}$ |
| S (1/3) | $\frac{K_r}{3}$  | -$\frac{K_p}{3}$ | 0                | $\frac{K_r}{3}-\frac{K_p}{3}$ |
| Total   | 0                | 0                | 0                | 0                             |
*Table for Player A's payoff using strategy $K$ against player B*

Thus, if both players play a uniform 1/3-1/3-1/3 distribution strategy for rock paper scissors, there is a Nash equilibrium as both players are never incentivised to change their strategy. 

To prove this is a unique solution, suppose there exists a strategy J that follows $0$ <= $J_r, J_s, J_p$ <= $1$ and $J_r + J_s + J_p = 1$, and $J_p$, $J_r$, and $J_s$ are not all $\frac{1}{3}$ (at least one of them are not $\frac{1}{3}$). From here we can construct a strategy $S$ which can be shown to have a positive expected payoff when used against J. 
$S_p$ = $J_r$
$S_r$ = $J_s$
$S_s$ = $J_p$

Here, the expected payoff for using strategy $S$ against strategy $J$ is given by ${J_r}^2+{J_s}^2+{J_p}^2-({J_p}\cdot{J_s}+{J_s}\cdot{J_r}+{J_r}\cdot{J_p})$

This is just proofs and inequalities:
let a = $J_r$, b = $J_s$, c = $J_p$

(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(a-c)^2 = 2(a^2+b^2+c^2)-2(ab+bc+ac)
LHS>=0, thus RHS>=0
a^2+b^2+c^2-(ab+bc+ac)>=0
AND
a^2+b^2+c^2-(ab+bc+ac)=0 when (a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(a-c)^2 = 0 which occurs when a-b=0 and b-c=0 and a-c=0(sum of squares). Thus, payoff is 0 only when a=b=c=$\frac{1}{3}$, and positive otherwise. 

From our assumption that $J$ is not the even $\frac{1}{3}$ distribution. In a single-item auction with at least three bidders where the payment rule is defined for the winner to be the third-highest bid, this auction is not dominant-strategy incentive compatible as a bidder's utility may be increased by increasing their bid. Suppose bidder i has a private valuation $v_i$ and is contemplating overbidding compared to bidding their true value. If we fix other bids $b$ and suppose there exists one bid $b_j$ where $b_j > v_i$, and the highest bid below $v_i$ is bid $b_k$ ($b_k<v_i$, and among all bids-i-j, $b_k$ is closest to $v_i$), then bidder i's utility from bidding $v_i$ is 0 as they are have the 2nd highest bid. However, if they bid $b_j+p$ where p is a positive amount, their utility is $v_i-b_k$. As $b_k$ is less than $v_i$ by definition, this introduces a scenario where the strategy to bid truthfully does not guarantee to maximise a bidder's utility, and thus the auction is not dominant-strategy incentive compatible.

The analog of the second-price auction for an auction with $k$ identical copies of a good and the given restrictions is to award one good for each of the $k$ highest bidders, and to charge each of the kth highest bidders the bid of the $k$+1th highest bidder. That is, each bidder that wins pays the bid of the highest losing bidder.

This auction is dominant-strategy incentive compatible as the dominant strategy to bid truthfully always maximises a bidder's utility.

Fixing $i$, $v_i$, and other bids $b$.

There are 2 cases:
1. valuation within the top k highest bids
2. valuation outside the top k highest bids

Case 1:
if $v_i$ is within the top k highest bids, then the maximum possible utility is $v_i$ - $b_{k+1}$, which is achieved by bidding $v_i$.

Case 2:
if $v_i$ is outside the top k highest bids, then the maximum possible utility is 0, which is achieved by setting $b_i = v_i$

Thus, in both cases, $i$'s utility is maximised by bidding truthfully, and therefore the auction is DSIC.

Let there be $n$ bidders with private valuations represented as vector $v$ of length $n$ and bids $b$ of length $n$.

An analog of the Vickrey auction for this case is an allocation rule that chooses the bidder with the lowest bid, and a payment rule that assigns to the bidder with the lowest bid the cost of the second lowest bidder.

In this auction, bidding truthfully is a dominant strategy as it maximises utility both in the case of winning and losing.

Fixing $i$, $v_i$, and other bids $b$:

Let B = min $b_j$, $j$ != $i$ of $b$

Case 1: $v_i>B$
Maximum utility = 0
achieved by bidding truthfully($b_i=v_i$)

Case 2: $v_i<B$
Maximum utility = $B-v_i$
achieved by bidding truthfully($b_i = v_i$)

Thus, a bidder never recieves a negative utility for bidding truthfully and it is also a dominant strategy. The auction also chooses the contractor with the lowest private cost as a part of it's allocation rule.
